Problem Framing – User Problems

The framing here follows the point-of-view format from the lectures — User + Need + Because (the insight that points toward a solution, not merely a reason for the problem) — reframed into a How Might We design challenge that stays open enough to admit more than one solution.

FORGE answers two questions with one instrument, so it serves two users. They are not redundant: the first asks a decision question with a cost attached (“more data, or am I done?”); the second asks an open how question (“what is driving this uncertainty, and what happens if I change it?”). The diagnostician is the motivating problem — it is what makes the work worth doing. The observatory is the capability problem — it is what the tool literally does, and what hypotheses H1/H2/H3 test. The diagnostic use is one application of the general instrument.

User 1 · Diagnostician

“Should I collect more data, or is this as good as it gets?”

How might we let a domain researcher see a model’s epistemic uncertainty so that they can decide whether more data would help — without making them build a Bayesian pipeline themselves?

User
Graduate researchers in data-rich physical sciences (HEP, condensed matter, materials) who train their own neural-net classifiers or regressors but are not Bayesian-ML specialists.
Need
a way to see whether a trained model’s confidence is earned or borrowed — whether its remaining error is epistemic (fixable with more or better data, or a different architecture) or irreducible —
Because
a single point estimate hides what the model does not know, and standing up a full Bayesian pipeline (HMC/NUTS, calibration, last-layer Laplace) from scratch is enough engineering that they skip it and ship overconfident models — so they cannot tell whether to keep investing in data or stop.

User 2 · Observatory

“What is driving this uncertainty, and what happens if I change it?”

How might we let someone reshape a posterior’s geometry in real time — by turning a prior, sampler, or architecture knob — so that they can build and test intuition about what drives a model’s uncertainty?

User
Practitioners and students working with Bayesian deep learning who want to understand how their modeling choices shape the posterior — but who currently iterate through a slow edit-code-and-wait loop with only trace plots and scalar diagnostics to look at.
Need
an interactive instrument to reshape the posterior in real time — turn a prior, a sampler setting, a likelihood, an architecture knob — and immediately see the geometry respond,
Because
the normal Bayesian loop is slow (minutes per HMC run) and its outputs are scalars and trace plots, not geometry you can steer — so intuition about how choices reshape the posterior is expensive to build and slow to test.

Why the insight clause matters

In both statements the Because is doing design-actionable work rather than restating the problem. Each one is the same fact that justifies a piece of the architecture, stated as a user pain: the cost of standing up a Bayesian pipeline (User 1) is exactly why FORGE preloads working baselines; the slowness of the edit-and-wait loop (User 2) is exactly why the LLLA closed-form fast path is non-negotiable for real-time knob responsiveness (hypothesis H3). The problem framing and the technical design are the same claim seen from two sides.

Market Research – Gauging Project Novelty

The market-research lecture asks one question: is the project new or novel? Because FORGE has both a software and a research element, novelty is gauged on two fronts — a scan of comparable tools and sites (what already exists, so the build is differentiated and clear of others' ground) and a literature review (how the underlying methods have been done before, so the contribution is genuinely new).

Method: search engines and LLMs for the software landscape; Google Scholar for the “research of research.” Each comparable below overlaps FORGE on at least one axis — interactive model exploration, posterior/uncertainty visualization, sampler diagnostics, or Bayesian experimentation — and each is paired with the line that separates it from FORGE.

Literature Search — research landscape

The “research of research” prong: peer-reviewed work that grounds FORGE's ingredients and sharpens where its contribution is new.

Reference Focus Relevance to FORGE
Dearden, Friedman & Andre — Model-based Bayesian Exploration (UAI, 1999) Maintains a posterior over MDP models and uses value-of-information to drive reinforcement-learning exploration, sampling Q-value distributions instead of point estimates. An early statement of the core FORGE logic — a posterior over models beats a point estimate for deciding where knowledge is lacking — but applied as an RL exploration algorithm, not a visualization tool.
Frazier — A Tutorial on Bayesian Optimization (2018) GP surrogate plus acquisition functions; a posterior over an expensive black box with credible intervals, balancing exploration and exploitation. Shares the “visualize posterior uncertainty, decide where you're unsure” pattern, but aimed at optimizing a function rather than diagnosing a trained model.
Wang, Jin, Schmitt & Olhofer — Recent Advances in Bayesian Optimization (ACM Comput. Surv., 2023) Comprehensive taxonomy of Bayesian optimization (GPs, acquisition functions, high-dimensional, multi-objective, multi-fidelity…). Maps the BO field and confirms it is optimization-centric — useful breadth for positioning FORGE's model-diagnosis purpose as distinct.
Gabry, Simpson, Vehtari, Betancourt & Gelman — Visualization in Bayesian Workflow (JRSS A, 2019) The canonical reference for visualization across the whole Bayesian workflow: prior predictive, HMC diagnostics, posterior-predictive checks, LOO; companion to bayesplot. The methodological backbone for FORGE's plots — but static, R-package workflow guidance rather than an interactive hosted dashboard.
Nussbaumer, Böck & Cito — Online and Interactive Bayesian Inference Debugging (ICSE '26); tool: InferLog Holmes An online (during-inference) and interactive MCMC debugger inside VSCode, with live trace/posterior views and automatic warnings. The closest prior art to FORGE's online, interactive ambition — but an IDE debugger for PPL code aimed at fixing inference, not a hosted multi-domain dashboard for exploring a trained model's epistemic uncertainty with knob-driven posterior reshaping and a deterministic-vs-Bayesian comparison.

Where FORGE is novel

The landscape splits cleanly: deterministic ML playgrounds, post-hoc posterior diagnostics (libraries), probabilistic-programming frameworks, production UQ monitors, and interactive sampler explainers on toy targets. The three closest interactive analogues each cover one slice — the MCMC gallery and the GP demo animate sampling on fixed targets or 1-D regression; InferLog Holmes debugs PPL inference live in an IDE. None is a hosted dashboard that simultaneously (1) preloads trained models across heterogeneous domains, (2) toggles deterministic ↔ Bayesian and compares posteriors against the baseline, and (3) reshapes posterior geometry in real time through a knob panel backed by closed-form LLLA plus heavy HMC. The literature grounds each ingredient — workflow visualization, posterior-uncertainty with acquisition, posterior-over-models for decisions, online interactive inference — but the combination, framed by “is this model's confidence earned or borrowed; has it reached its epistemic limit?”, is the differentiated contribution.

Digital Ethics – Harm Evaluation

Lecture 3 · full table due with proposal

This lecture inverts the usual question. Instead of asking what the project does well, it asks what harm it could cause — the worst case if it shipped and people, or an attacker, used it in ways never intended. The instrument is a checklist: each concern is answered Yes / No / Maybe for ordinary use and again under a data breach, and anything that is not a clean “No” gets a written mitigation — whether or not the team would actually build it.

The prompts the lecture poses are the right ones to sit with: how could the product be misused to harm others; how is the data stored, and what happens if it leaks or is hacked; how sensitive is what is collected, and would participants object to its collection at all; and are there age or vulnerability concerns — what happens when a child, or someone with diminished capacity, uses it. The worked example is Craigslist, where ordinary-use answers are uncomfortably affirmative (illegal-item sales, sex trafficking, academic cheating all Yes; a stalker locating someone Maybe) and each is paired with a concrete mitigation — keyword flagging with an appeal path, explicit policy warnings, and making location optional rather than mandatory.

FORGE is a far smaller attack surface. It is a hosted dashboard for exploring preloaded Bayesian posteriors of already-trained models across three scientific domains. There is no marketplace, no messaging, no user-to-user contact, no profiles, no location data, and no camera or microphone access; the only per-user state is saved model configurations and posterior samples, scoped to the owning account. Against the checklist, almost every row is a clean “No”. The lecture also asks us to connect a real-world example to the project: the closest analog is the spying / phone-tracking case (row 5) — and FORGE sidesteps it by construction, since it collects no data about third parties to track in the first place.

FORGE harm checklist

# Concern Normal use Data breach Mitigation / note
1 Could a user sell drugs or other illegal items on the platform? No No FORGE is not a marketplace — nothing is listed, bought, or sold. No user-generated commerce exists to police.
2 Could a user engage in sex trafficking via the platform? No No No listings, profiles, messaging, or contact channels between users; there is no surface through which people interact at all.
3 Could a user sell class notes or cheat on homework on the platform? Maybe No Not a content-exchange platform, so nothing is sold. The only thin edge is academic integrity — a student presenting FORGE-generated analysis as their own. Mitigated by FORGE being a documented, attributable research tool, not an answer service; outputs are reproducible and traceable to the preloaded baseline.
4 Could a stalker use the project to find someone? No No No personal data, no locations, and no user directory are collected or displayed; there is nothing locatable.
5 Could the app be used to spy on or track individuals? No No No tracking of any kind. Per-user saved artifacts are account-scoped model configurations and posterior samples — data about models, never about third parties.
6 Could the software access the camera or microphone and record without users being aware? No No A browser dashboard that requests no camera or microphone permissions; there is no capture path to abuse.
7 Could someone using the platform be re-traumatized or have their mental health impacted? No No Content is purely technical — audio features, crystal structures, particle-physics classifiers. Nothing is distressing, personal, or targeted at vulnerable users.

Data governance – the one open item

The substantive consideration for FORGE is not in the harm rows but in data provenance. Two of the three datasets are public — GTZAN audio features and the Materials Project / DFPT phonon data. The third, ATLAS detector data, is governed by ATLAS / CERN data-release policy, and whether the underlying detector data may be shown on a public site is still to be determined. The mitigation is simple and already identified: the site does not need to display the detector data at all. FORGE can serve the trained model, its posteriors, and derived plots while keeping the raw detector data off the public site entirely — which also bounds breach exposure, since the per-user store then holds only low-sensitivity model configurations and posterior samples.

Note (N. Herling): “Full release of the detector data is yet to be determined, but could be solved by simply not displaying the data on the website. The further ethical concerns appear minimal — detector data is used to solve physics problems about the constituents of matter.”

Status: the complete ethical-considerations table lives in the project proposal and will be formally evaluated at proposal submission — about 1.5 weeks out (mid-June 2026). The evaluation above is the initial synopsis; the proposal table is the system of record.

Timeline & Gantt Chart

Lecture 4 · implemented in repo

Two views of the same schedule. A timeline is built by working backward from the finished deliverable, laying every step into the weeks it has to land in. A Gantt chart reverse-engineers that timeline into a project-management view — interconnected tasks in a waterfall, with done vs. outstanding legible at a glance — and is refreshed at every weekly check-in.

The lecture’s emphasis is accountability: the timeline is the document the project is graded against — what was promised against what was delivered — and the act of placing milestones is itself a scope reality-check, surfacing a plan that is too big, too small, or quietly slipping on one person before it becomes a crisis. The Gantt adds the interconnection the timeline lacks: a blocking dependency (you cannot start the next task until this one finishes) reads differently from an independent slip that costs nothing downstream. In the worked example, completed work is greyed out and behind-schedule work is flagged red, so the chart doubles as a live checklist.

Both are already built into this repository, so this section is a pointer rather than new work. The Project Timeline panel carries the 13-week schedule, phase-color-coded and anchored to the Week 0 start (18 May 2026); the Gantt Chart panel renders the same tasks interactively with per-task status (done / in-progress / outstanding) and the dependency flow across weeks. Beyond the lecture’s ask, the Gantt was extended with a per-week to-do layer — a hand-edited weekly_todos structure that expands each week in the chart’s detail panel into individual tasks marked todo or done, giving the finer granularity the weekly check-ins run against.

Note (N. Herling): “Timeline and Gantt are already handled in the repo. I added a per-week to-do structure for more granularity — it surfaces in the Gantt detail panel and is updated at each weekly check-in.”
